#############################################
The most important issue: the business model
#############################################

From a technical perspective many of these ideas make sense. Yet in practise things don’t quite work out as expected. Why? My take, coming from what I have studied and personal working experience, is that what usually appears as a technology problem, it is actually a business problem. Obviously, I am not the first person to say that; but it is definitely and idea that can help us understand how Agile is applied in the real world.

Imagine for a moment a business that optimises for cost reduction. Can this business successfully apply Agile? Of course not. Most of the technical practices, like refactoring, will immediately go out of the window. What we will get is spaghetti code, produced in sprints instead of phases. Not much of a difference.
Imagine for a moment a business that thrives on a culture of micro-management and favouritism. Can this business successfully apply Agile? Of course not. Deciding technical matters following the HIPPO (Highest Person’s Personal Opinion) principle will get us spaghetti code, produced in sprints instead of phases. Not much of a difference either.
In general, the fundamental question is: what do we optimise for? If quality is not a business objective then on the one hand it does not matter which methodology we use to create a product, and on the other Agile is not even feasible.
What are the conditions for quality not being a business objective? Well, there are many. Let’s start with ownership structure. 

Imagine a private company that successfully applies Agile, which then goes public. The stockholders’ pressure for better financial result, can pretty easily push management to cut corners on technical excellence.

Imagine a private company that successfully applies Agile, which is then sold to a private fund. The fund has the business plan of doubling the asset’s value and selling in 3 to 5 years to materialise this value. The easiest way to do so is to cut costs, drive expensive engineers out, and maybe outsource engineering to other countries. This will reduce costs and increase technical debt. The latter though does not appear in any financial statement and will remain hidden until someone buys the company. In such scenario, Agile goes out of the window.

A similar situation can arise in a scenario with a relatively simple product in a highly competitive market. Think of games for mobile devices. In this situation, time to market and throughput are essential for implementing a strategy of product differentiation and market saturation. If any competitor publishes a game, the aim is to create multiple similar games so that each one differentiates a bit and all of them together saturate the market. Even if the original game is better, the differentiation can chip away users, and the mass creates volume of users for our company. In that situation, the objective is to simply churn out games, and Agile is mostly irrelevant.
